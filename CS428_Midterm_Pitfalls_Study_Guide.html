How to cite this guide accurately

Each entry separates what the reading says from the study-guide interpretation, the invented scenario, and the proposed response. This matters because the source may support the concept without containing the exact example or solution wording shown here.

Best citation practice: cite the original reading for the definition, observation, case, or recommendation. If you use the hypothetical example below, describe it as your own illustration rather than attributing it to Webster.
  • Web articles: use the article title, author (Bruce F. Webster, unless co-authored), publication date, and the named paragraph/section locator supplied below. The webpages generally do not number paragraphs, so use a heading or the opening words of the paragraph rather than inventing page numbers.
  • Systems Failure PDF: use the printed page number shown in the document (15-page report), section title, and pattern number. Example: “Patterns in IT Litigation: Systems Failure, p. 3, ‘Pattern 1: Faulty Towers’.”
  • BFWA Pitfalls index: it is a list of the names and categories of pitfalls. The index alone is not a detailed explanation of every item. Where no linked individual pitfall page was separately reviewed, the summary below is explicitly a concise interpretation of the title, not a quotation or detailed claim from Webster.
  • Solutions: where the source gives recommendations, they are identified as source-based. Where a solution is a reasonable application of the principle, it is labeled “study-guide inference.”

2. People, talent, and organizational communication

Wetware Crisis and TEPES

PeopleTalent and capability

Core idea: the limiting resource in software development can be capable people, not simply machines, tools, or headcount. Webster argues that there are not enough highly capable IT engineers and that inherent talent is unevenly distributed.

TEPES: Talent, Education, Professionalism, Experience, Skills. Treat these as different dimensions of a person's contribution; a credential or job title does not prove all five.

Illustration: management assumes doubling a team will double output, but new hires need onboarding and coordination and may not have the experience required for the hard technical work.

Response: hire and assign for demonstrated capability; develop skills; retain experienced staff; don't treat developers as interchangeable units. These are practical implications of the people-quality argument.

Source locator: The Wetware Crisis: TEPES, Jan. 10, 2008, opening paragraphs (especially the paragraph beginning “In my forthcoming book…”). The page states the shortage argument and introduces inherent talent. Also see “The Real Software Crisis”, Sept. 13, 2013 repost of BYTE Jan. 1996, opening paragraphs: shortage of top-notch programmers and the importance of people quality.

Dead Sea Effect

PeopleRetention

Core idea: organizations may retain less capable employees while their strongest people leave. Webster introduces the analogy by describing a system where water enters but leaves through evaporation, leaving a more concentrated, inhospitable environment.

Illustration: strong developers repeatedly leave because they are frustrated or have better opportunities; replacements do not restore the same level of expertise, so the organization’s overall capability erodes.

Response: address why effective people leave; provide meaningful work, growth, recognition, and a healthy environment. Goal alignment is a related retention strategy discussed in the later article.

Source locator: The Wetware Crisis: the Dead Sea effect, Apr. 11, 2008, paragraphs beginning “There are many reasons…” and “Many large corporate/government IT shops…”. For retention and goal-alignment response: How to retain IT talent with goal alignment, Sept. 16, 2013, paragraphs beginning “It was the start of what would be a three-year grind…” and “I took the software engineers…”.

The Longest Yard: Reorganizing IT for Success

PeopleOrganization

Core idea: managing skilled technical workers like interchangeable industrial-age labor ignores the nature of knowledge work. The article argues that talent management is a key success factor and organizational practices need to evolve.

Illustration: a team is managed as if developers are replaceable production-line workers; assignments, motivation, and working relationships are neglected.

Response: manage skilled people as skilled people: develop talent, assign work thoughtfully, support teamwork, and adapt organizational practices. These are study-guide implications; cite the article for the broader talent-management argument.

Source locator: The Longest Yard: Reorganizing IT for Success, Apr. 14, 2008, opening paragraph beginning “Managing talent and skilled workers…”. Co-authored by Ruby Raley; originally printed in the September 2006 Cutter IT Journal.

Thermocline of Truth

PeopleProject visibility

Core idea: a barrier in the organizational chart separates people who know the project's real status from leaders who receive a more optimistic picture. Webster explicitly connects this to missing objective, repeatable metrics and informal “80% done” estimates.

Illustration: developers know integration is failing, but executives hear that the project is nearly complete.

Response: use objective, repeatable measurements; surface risks and blockers; make status reporting safe and evidence-based; validate completion in a production-like environment.

Source locator: The Wetware Crisis: the Thermocline of Truth, Apr. 15, 2008, paragraphs beginning “In many large or even medium-sized IT projects…” and “Several major (and mutually reinforcing) factors…”.

Goal Alignment and IT Talent Retention

PeopleRetention

Core idea: understand what employees hope to gain personally from the work and find overlap with team and organizational objectives. The article describes an off-site discussion of engineers' personal goals as part of retaining a team through a difficult product-development period.

Illustration: one engineer wants to deepen technical expertise while another values leadership experience; management can create work opportunities that connect those goals to product needs.

Response: discuss individual goals openly, create shared goals, and use alignment to sustain commitment. The specific scenario is illustrative.

Source locator: How to retain IT talent with goal alignment, Sept. 16, 2013, paragraphs beginning “It was the start of what would be a three-year grind…” and “I took the software engineers…”.

Negotiations and Lovesongs: Introduction

PeopleIncentives and methodology

Core idea: software development in corporations and government is a multi-player situation involving people and groups with different interests. The article opens with a disagreement about whether methodologies are ineffective or simply badly applied, then frames organizational development through game theory.

Illustration: a project has a technically sound process, but departments pursue conflicting incentives and do not cooperate.

Response: understand stakeholder incentives and the interactions among groups; don't assume a methodology alone resolves organizational conflict. This response is an interpretation of the introduction, not a verbatim checklist from the article.

Source locator: Negotiations and Lovesongs: Introduction, Apr. 16, 2008, paragraphs beginning “I used to have arguments…” and “Then while watching the movie…”.

3. Project execution, metrics, and delivery

Anatomy of a Runaway IT Project

Project executionCase study

Core idea: a major mission-critical re-engineering effort ran for years, cost millions, and was eventually canceled with work products abandoned. The article reproduces a redacted project-review memo and reports concerns about quality of deliverables and the feasibility of production deployment.

Illustration from the reading: the reviewed project had been underway for several years; the memo records consultants' doubts about whether the project as pursued could ever reach successful production deployment.

Response: independent review, professional standards for deliverables, honest risk reporting, and a decision to redirect or stop a project when evidence shows the current path is not viable. The PDF Systems Failure reading gives a more explicit proceed/redirect/cancel framework.

Source locator: Anatomy of a runaway IT project, June 16, 2008, opening paragraphs and “CONFIDENTIAL MEMORANDUM — EYES ONLY,” especially “QUALITY OF WORK AND EFFORT.”

Lies, Damned Lies, and Project Metrics (Parts I–III)

Project executionMeasurement

Core idea: inaccurate metrics and inadequate measurement can seriously mislead IT project management. An ideal metric should be informative, preferably predictive, objective, and automated. Part II explains that prediction concerns the gap between the current system and what is needed to ship. Part III introduces instrumentation and heuristics as ways to monitor and predict project course.

Illustration: a manager counts tasks or lines of code and reports progress, while defects, incomplete integration, and remaining work are not measured.

Response: measure meaningful outcomes and project state; collect data systematically; use metrics consistently; combine instrumentation with heuristics. Do not assume one simplistic number captures project health.

Source locator: Part I, July 8, 2013, opening paragraphs defining metrics and explaining risk; Part II, July 11, 2013, paragraphs beginning “In my previous post…” and “A predictive IT project metric…”; Part III, July 12, 2013, opening paragraph introducing instrumentation and heuristics.

Do Not Defer the Difficult

Project executionRisk

Core idea: teams naturally work on easy, visible tasks to demonstrate progress. A nearly complete-looking UI can exist before application functionality. Teams may also leave a subsystem at about 80% when it becomes difficult, then move to easier work elsewhere.

Illustration: the demo looks polished, but core integration, performance, and complex business logic are still unresolved.

Response: identify the hardest and riskiest work early; prototype and integrate early; don't use visible UI progress as a proxy for system completion. Early prototyping/integration is a study-guide recommendation derived from the problem.

Source locator: Do not defer the difficult in IT projects, Aug. 26, 2013, paragraphs beginning “When an IT project starts…” and “Such tasks often include the user interface…”.

Septic Code

Project executionCode quality

Core idea: a portion of source code can be so defective and so central to dependent code that the project cannot stabilize or move toward production until that code is removed wholesale and replaced with professionally built code. Webster distinguishes this from merely poorly written code.

Illustration: multiple modules depend on a critical component whose behavior is unreliable; patches to dependent modules cannot make the whole system stable.

Response: identify the central defective component, assess dependencies, and replace it rather than endlessly patching around it. This follows the article's explicit description of cutting out and replacing the code.

Source locator: Septic code: why some large IT projects never go into production, Sept. 12, 2013, paragraphs beginning “A common pattern…” and “By that, I mean…”.

Never-Ending Story / Runaway Project

Project executionSystems Failure pattern 4

Core idea: delivery dates repeatedly slip until the vendor abandons the work, the client cancels, or an inadequate system is delivered. The Systems Failure paper identifies unclear, unstable, unconstrained requirements and lack of qualified technical managers/developers as recurring causes.

Illustration: each revised deadline passes without a deployable system, while costs continue to rise.

Response: stabilize and constrain requirements; ensure qualified technical leadership; use staged delivery; document risks; decide whether to proceed, redirect, scale back, or cancel.

Source locator: Systems Failure PDF, p. 6, “Pattern 4: The Never-Ending Story,” especially summary, causes, and recommendations (PDF text lines 190–207 onward). Also Septic code, Sept. 12, 2013, opening section describing the pattern.

4. Architecture, maintenance, and technology lifecycle

Arc of Engineering

Architecture and lifecycle

Core idea: system quality rises over time to a peak and then begins to decline. The article explicitly names this pattern the “arc of engineering.”

Illustration: continued changes after a system has reached a strong, stable state introduce complexity and defects that reduce quality.

Response: evaluate whether changes improve the system; protect stability; avoid equating more change or more features with better quality. This response is a practical inference from the pattern.

Source locator: The Arc of Engineering, May 21, 2008, paragraph beginning “I have observed a pattern…” and the paragraph defining the rise-to-peak-then-decline curve.

Software Rot and the Maintenance Architect

Architecture and lifecycleMaintenance

Core idea: software becomes fragile and harder to support as changes are made with little analysis of secondary effects. Interdependencies can cause a fix in one system to ripple into others. The article also observes that maintenance may be assigned to entry-level or less effective engineers, contributing to declining quality.

Illustration: a quick patch fixes one bug but breaks another subsystem; more patches create a fragile network of workarounds.

Response: treat maintenance as architectural work, analyze change impact, coordinate dependencies, and provide experienced oversight. The article's title proposes a maintenance architect; cite the article for the role and rationale.

Source locator: Controlling IT Costs: Using a Maintenance Architect, July 22, 2013, paragraphs beginning “Software rots over time” and “When a defect is fixed…”.

Technology Lifecycle Mismatch: Four Patterns

Architecture and lifecycleTechnology selection

Core idea: technologies age at different rates, and a technology's product lifecycle may not align with the organization's business or development lifecycle.

PatternMeaningScenario clue
FireflyLooks promising but remains inadequate; each new release seems to promise a fix, keeping the organization waiting.“The next version will finally work.”
UnderdoneTechnology is immature or not yet sufficiently stable.Early release requires the next corrective release.
Conveyor BeltTechnology has a useful but limited lifespan; it may be carried out of viability during the project.Support or useful life will end before project value is realized.
LandfillTechnology is effectively obsolete or has little future viability.Adoption begins with an already-declining or dead-end choice.

Response: compare technology maturity and lifespan with the time horizon of the project; check support, market viability, upgrade paths, and exit/migration options.

Source locator: Getting technology lifecycles in sync, Aug. 23, 2013, opening paragraphs on different lifecycle rates and the section headings “Firefly Technologies,” “Underdone Technologies,” “Conveyor Belt Technologies,” and “Landfill Technologies.”

Conway's Law

Architecture and lifecycleOrganization design

Core idea: the architecture of a system tends to copy the communication structure of the organization that builds it. Webster's QUBE case describes organizational silos, fragmented testing, barriers to communication, and a similarly balkanized architecture.

Illustration: departments build separate components without coordination, and the resulting system has awkward boundaries and integration problems.

Response: align team communication and responsibilities with desired architecture; create cross-team coordination and coherent QA/testing structures.

Source locator: Remember Conway’s Law, Oct. 8, 2013, QUBE case and quoted executive-summary passage; the article quotes the law explicitly in the paragraph beginning “Any organization that designs a system…”.

Microsoft Windows Forever and Ever?

Architecture and lifecycleLegacy technology

Core idea: a highly successful and widely adopted platform can create a legacy that is difficult to move away from. Compatibility and installed-base dependence can make change difficult even when the platform ages.

Illustration: an organization depends on applications and workflows tied to a mature platform, making migration costly and risky.

Response: plan for compatibility and eventual migration; weigh the value of a stable installed base against long-term dependency. This is a study-guide interpretation of the article's legacy discussion.

Source locator: “Microsoft Windows Forever and Ever?”, Sept. 18, 2013 repost, opening paragraphs and the discussion beginning “Is there such a thing as too much success?”. Note: this is a 2013 repost of a Windows Magazine article from June 1996.

5. Systems Failure: six recurring patterns

The report analyzes 120 IT litigation cases from 1976–2000 and says the patterns overlap; a single case may exhibit two or three. The paper also cautions that its survey is neither exhaustive nor definitive.

Source locator: Patterns in IT Litigation: Systems Failure (1976–2000), Abstract and “A Survey of IT Litigation: Systems Failure,” printed pp. 1–2.

1. Faulty Towers

Client buys a system, then claims it is defective: errors, crashes, or unsatisfactory operation.

Response: define acceptance and quality standards; document defects and repair obligations.

Citation: PDF p. 3, “Pattern 1: Faulty Towers,” summary, causes, recommendations.

2. Irrational Exuberance

Vendor makes unrealistic promises and/or client expects a “silver bullet” that solves broad IT problems.

Response: agree on written definitions, specifications, and timetables; make sure both sides understand what is promised.

Citation: PDF pp. 4–5, “Pattern 2: Irrational Exuberance,” especially recommendations.

3. Three's a Crowd

Third parties such as leasing companies, consultants, or value-added resellers complicate responsibility and disputes.

Response: clarify what each party provides, warrants, recommends, and is responsible for.

Citation: PDF p. 5, “Pattern 3: Three’s a Crowd,” summary and recommendations.

4. Never-Ending Story

Delivery dates keep slipping until the vendor abandons, client cancels, or an unacceptable system is delivered.

Response: stable constrained requirements, qualified technical managers/developers, staged scope, and explicit recovery decisions.

Citation: PDF pp. 5–6, “Pattern 4: The Never-Ending Story.”

5. Unplanned Obsolescence

A system or product becomes unavailable, unsupported, or obsolete unexpectedly, often after vendor decisions.

Response: migration paths, support commitments, and contingency plans for technologies the client does not control.

Citation: PDF p. 6, “Pattern 5: Unplanned Obsolescence.”

6. Unintended Consequences

A vendor changes functionality or configuration in a way that harms existing clients or systems.

Response: impact analysis, thorough testing, limited rollout, and rapid correction.

Citation: PDF pp. 6–7, “Pattern 6: Unintended Consequences.”

Quality citation: Systems Failure PDF, printed p. 2, section “The Core Issue: Quality,” bullets for the eight attributes.

Four lessons from the report

  1. Get expert legal and IT guidance before signing.
  2. Specify critical terms and protections in writing.
  3. Act quickly when a project is in serious trouble; document risks and choose to proceed, redirect, scale back, or cancel.
  4. Remember that new technology entails risks, including the possibility that the proposed system is not technically feasible.
Source locator: Systems Failure PDF, printed pp. 7–8, “Lessons to Learn,” Lessons 1–4.

7. Practice quiz

Choose an answer, then reveal the explanation. Questions are original practice scenarios, not quotations from the readings.

1. Strong developers keep leaving, and the remaining organization becomes less capable.
Correct: Dead Sea Effect. The defining clue is the loss of the strongest people and the resulting change in organizational competence. Source: Dead Sea effect, Apr. 11, 2008, paragraphs describing the organizational analogy.
2. Executives believe a project is 80% complete, but developers know the real status is much worse.
Correct: Thermocline of Truth. The clue is a barrier to accurate progress information. Source: Thermocline article, Apr. 15, 2008, paragraphs 20–23.
3. A team demos a polished UI while the core functionality and integration are unfinished.
Correct: Do Not Defer the Difficult. The article specifically warns that a UI can look nearly complete before the application has functionality. Source: Aug. 26, 2013, opening paragraphs.
4. A central component is so defective that dependent code cannot stabilize until it is replaced.
Correct: Septic Code. The article distinguishes this from ordinary poor design: the critical code must be removed wholesale. Source: Sept. 12, 2013, paragraphs beginning “By that, I mean…”.
5. Several teams work in silos, and the system architecture mirrors those communication barriers.
Correct: Conway's Law. Webster's QUBE case links organizational silos and barriers to communication with balkanized architecture. Source: Oct. 8, 2013, QUBE case and quoted law.
6. A technology looks promising, but each release is flawed and the next release is always expected to fix it.
Correct: Firefly technology. Source: Getting technology lifecycles in sync, Aug. 23, 2013, “Firefly Technologies.”
7. A vendor modifies a product without sufficient impact analysis or testing, harming existing clients.
Correct: Unintended Consequences. Systems Failure PDF, pp. 6–7, Pattern 6 and recommendations.
8. A project repeatedly misses revised delivery dates and eventually gets canceled or delivers an unacceptable system.
Correct: Never-Ending Story. Systems Failure PDF, pp. 5–6, Pattern 4.
